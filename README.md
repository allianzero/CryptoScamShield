# CryptoScamShield

A practical guide to recognising, avoiding, and recovering from crypto scams. Crypto scams succeed not by breaking blockchains, but by tricking people — through fake sites, impersonation, urgency, and promises that are too good to be true. This guide teaches the patterns, so you can spot them before you act.

> This guide is educational only, not financial or legal advice. If you have been scammed, see section 8 for immediate steps — and beware of "recovery" scams that target victims a second time.

## 1. Why crypto scams work so well

Four properties of crypto make scams unusually effective:

1. **Irreversibility:** once you send crypto to a scammer, there is generally no bank to reverse it.
2. **Pseudonymity:** scammers operate behind addresses and accounts that are hard to trace and easy to replace.
3. **Complexity:** wallets, approvals, bridges, and contracts are confusing, and confusion is a scammer's best tool.
4. **Hype and greed:** bull markets create urgency and fear of missing out, which override caution.

Scammers do not need to be sophisticated. They need you to act quickly, without verifying, on a link or a promise. Every defence in this guide is, at heart, a way to slow down and verify.

## 2. The scam patterns: know them by name

### Phishing
A fake copy of a real site, app, or message, designed to steal your login, seed phrase, or approval.

- **Fake login pages:** identical to a real exchange or wallet site, reached from a link in an email, post, or DM. You type your password; the attacker captures it and drains your account.
- **Fake wallet sites:** ask you to "connect", "restore", or "verify" your wallet, then copy your seed phrase or trick you into a draining approval.
- **Fake apps:** wallet or exchange apps in app stores that look real but steal keys on first use.

**Defence:** only log in through bookmarks you created or the official app. Never log in from a link, even if the sender looks familiar — hacked accounts are used to phish their contacts.

### Impersonation and fake support
Someone pretends to be a project team member, an exchange employee, an influencer, or a friend.

- **Fake support DMs:** "support" contacts you first, offering to fix a problem. Real support never DMs first, and never asks for your seed phrase, password, or a remote-access session.
- **Hacked friend accounts:** a friend's account sends you a crypto link or asks for a loan. Verify through a second channel (a call, a different app) before acting.
- **Celebrity and influencer impersonation:** fake accounts with similar names and stolen photos promote giveaways or investments. Check handles character by character, and remember that real celebrities do not give away crypto for sending them crypto.

### Giveaway and doubling scams
"Send 1 ETH, get 2 ETH back." "First 1,000 participants double their money." Often promoted with fake livestreams, fake celebrity endorsements, and countdown timers.

**Truth:** no legitimate project, exchange, or person doubles your money for sending it to them. Ever. This pattern is always a scam, without exception.

### Fake investments and Ponzi schemes
A platform or "trading bot" promises guaranteed, high, steady returns — for example, "2% per day, guaranteed" — paid from new investors' deposits, not from real trading or revenue.

- Early participants may actually get paid, which makes the scheme look legitimate and attracts more victims.
- When new deposits slow, payments stop, the operators disappear, and later investors lose everything.
- Warning signs: guaranteed returns, no verifiable trading or revenue, referral bonuses for recruiting others, and pressure to reinvest rather than withdraw.

### Fake airdrops and tokens
- **Airdrop scam:** a token appears in your wallet, and its website asks you to connect your wallet to "claim" a larger amount. Connecting triggers a draining approval.
- **Fake token sale:** a presale for a token that does not exist, or a copy of a real token with a slightly different contract address, sold on a fake site.
- **Honeypot token:** a token you can buy but cannot sell, because the contract blocks selling (except for the creator). Its price chart looks like a rocket — because nobody can exit.

**Defence:** do not interact with unknown tokens in your wallet. Hide or ignore them. Verify every token's contract address from official sources before buying.

### Rug pulls
A project's team removes liquidity, mints unlimited tokens, or drains a treasury, then disappears — after attracting buyers with marketing and hype.

- **Liquidity rug:** the team removes the liquidity pool, so holders cannot sell.
- **Mint rug:** the team mints huge new supply and sells it, crashing the price.
- **Treasury rug:** the team drains a DAO or project treasury they controlled.

**Defence:** check whether liquidity is locked or burned, whether minting is possible and who controls it, and whether the treasury is controlled by a multisig or a vote — before buying (see CryptoAuditingGuide).

### Romance and pig-butchering scams
A scammer builds a personal relationship over weeks or months (dating apps, social media, messaging), then introduces a "crypto investment opportunity" — usually a fake trading platform showing fake profits. Victims are encouraged to deposit more and more, then cannot withdraw.

This is currently one of the most financially devastating scam categories. The relationship is the scam's delivery mechanism; the fake platform is its payload. If someone you met online introduces crypto investing, assume it is a scam until overwhelming evidence proves otherwise, and never deposit on a platform they recommended.

### Fake job, task, and recovery scams
- **Fake jobs:** "earn crypto by completing tasks" — you deposit a fee or "activate" an account, then the job disappears.
- **Recovery scams:** after a victim loses funds, a "recovery service" contacts them offering to retrieve the stolen crypto for an upfront fee. It is a second scam, targeting people already hurt. Legitimate recovery is rare, slow, and never requires an upfront fee to a stranger.

## 3. The anatomy of a scam message

Most scam messages contain several of these elements. Two or more together mean stop and verify:

- **Urgency:** "act now", "last chance", "your account will be closed today".
- **Authority:** a logo, a famous name, a "support" badge, or official-looking language.
- **Secrecy:** "don't tell anyone", "keep this between us", "don't check with support".
- **A link or QR code:** taking you somewhere you did not choose.
- **A request for keys, passwords, or approvals:** seed phrase, private key, login code, or a wallet approval you do not understand.
- **A promise:** guaranteed returns, doubling, free money, or exclusive access.
- **A payment first:** a fee, tax, or deposit required before you can withdraw, claim, or "activate" something.

**The pause rule:** if a message makes you feel rushed, excited, or afraid, pause for 30 seconds and verify through an independent channel before doing anything. Scams depend on speed; verification depends on slowness.

## 4. Verifying before you act: a checklist

Before sending funds, connecting a wallet, or approving anything, check:

- [ ] **Source:** did I reach this site/app through a bookmark or official app I chose — or through a link someone sent me?
- [ ] **Domain:** is the web address exactly right, character by character? Lookalike letters and extra words are classic tricks.
- [ ] **Contract address:** for any token or protocol, does the address match the project's official site exactly?
- [ ] **Request:** is anyone asking for my seed phrase, password, or private key? (If yes, it is a scam. Stop.)
- [ ] **Approval:** what exactly am I approving, and to whom? If I do not understand it, I do not sign it.
- [ ] **Promise:** are returns guaranteed, or does it sound too good to be true? (If yes, it is.)
- [ ] **Second channel:** can I verify this through an independent source — the project's official site, a known community channel, or a person I trust, contacted separately?

If any check fails, do not proceed. There will always be another opportunity; stolen funds do not come back.

## 5. Wallet and account hygiene (scam prevention)

- Seed phrase on paper only, offline, never photographed, never shared.
- Unique passwords and authenticator-app 2FA on exchange and email.
- A separate, low-value wallet for experimenting with new sites, mints, and airdrops. Never connect your main savings wallet to something untested.
- Regular approval reviews and revocations.
- Bookmarks for the real sites you use; no logins from links.
- Unknown tokens in your wallet: ignore, hide, never interact.

See WalletSetupGuide and CryptoSecurityBasics for the full habits behind each of these.

## 6. Spotting fake projects before you buy

Quick audit before any purchase (full version in CryptoAuditingGuide):

1. Contract verified? Audits public and recent? Admin controls understood?
2. Tokenomics public and matching on-chain data? Unlocks checked?
3. Team known and accountable, or anonymous with no oversight?
4. Real usage over months, or only launch-week hype?
5. Liquidity locked/burned, or removable by the team?
6. Community able to criticise, or critics banned?

A project failing several of these is not "early" — it is risky. Price charts cannot compensate for failed fundamentals.

## 7. Common scam phrases, decoded

| What they say | What it means |
|---|---|
| "Send 1, get 2 back" | You will send 1 and get 0 back |
| "Guaranteed 2% daily" | A Ponzi scheme, or a lie |
| "Your wallet needs verification" | They want your seed phrase or a draining approval |
| "Support will DM you" | Real support does not DM first |
| "Don't tell anyone about this opportunity" | They do not want you to be warned |
| "Pay a tax/fee to unlock your withdrawal" | Advance-fee fraud; paying once leads to more demands, never a withdrawal |
| "This is a secret presale, only for you" | Mass-targeted phishing, personalised to feel exclusive |
| "I can recover your stolen crypto for a fee" | A second scam targeting victims |

## 8. If you have been scammed: immediate steps

Act quickly, in this order:

1. **Stop sending anything.** Do not pay "fees", "taxes", or "recovery" charges. Each payment is a new loss.
2. **Move remaining funds to a fresh wallet** with a brand-new seed phrase, if your wallet, seed phrase, or approvals may be compromised. Do this before anything else.
3. **Revoke approvals** for any suspicious contracts, from a clean device.
4. **Change passwords and re-check 2FA** on your exchange and email, starting with email.
5. **Preserve evidence:** transaction hashes, wallet addresses, website URLs, screenshots, chat logs, and dates. Do not delete anything.
6. **Report it:**
   - To the exchange or platform involved, if any.
   - To your local police / cybercrime unit.
   - To relevant fraud reporting services in your country (for example, the FTC in the US, Action Fraud in the UK, or your national equivalent).
   - To the project being impersonated, if applicable, so they can warn others.
7. **Warn your contacts** if your account was used to target them.
8. **Accept the likely outcome honestly.** Recovery of stolen crypto is rare. Anyone promising otherwise for a fee is almost certainly scamming you again. Focus on preventing the next loss, and on reporting so others are warned.

## 9. Helping others avoid scams

- Share this guide, not just warnings. People ignore "don't get scammed" but remember patterns and phrases.
- When a friend shares a "great opportunity", ask the verification questions in section 4 together, without mocking them. Shame makes victims hide; curiosity makes them check.
- Report scam sites, accounts, and addresses to platforms and block explorers where reporting is available. Each report makes the next victim slightly less likely.

## 10. Frequently asked questions

**Can the police / exchange reverse a crypto scam transaction?**
Almost always no. Crypto transactions are generally irreversible. Exchanges can sometimes freeze funds if they are still on the exchange and reported very quickly, but once funds move to private wallets, recovery is extremely unlikely.

**Are all new tokens scams?**
No, but most new tokens fail, and a large share are outright scams. Treat every new token as guilty until its contract, team, tokenomics, and usage prove otherwise.

**Is a project safe if it has many followers?**
No. Followers can be bought, bots are cheap, and influencers are paid. Follower count measures marketing spend, not legitimacy.

**What if a scammer knows my wallet address?**
An address alone is not enough to steal funds — addresses are public by design. The danger is if they also have your seed phrase, password, or an approval from you. If they have only your address, do not engage, and do not send them anything.
